Lesson 3 · 50 min

Microscopes and scale

Students convert between millimetres, micrometres and nanometres, calculate the field of view of the virtual microscope, and use it to estimate the size of real pond organisms.

  • Middle school (NGSS MS)
  • Ontario Grade 11 Biology (SBI3U)
  • Scale and microscopes
  • Microorganisms

Student worksheet Answer key (teachers)

Learning goals

  • Convert between millimetres, micrometres and nanometres.
  • Calculate field of view from the field number and the objective lens.
  • Estimate an organism's size from how much of the field it fills, and check it against the field guide.
  • Explain the difference between magnification and resolving power.

You need

  • One device per pair
  • Calculators
  • Student worksheet 3

5 min Hook

Play the home zoom from start to end. The first view is 0.6 mm across and the last is 0.4 nanometres across. Ask students to guess how many times smaller the last view is, and write the guesses down. (Answer: 0.0006 m divided by 0.0000000004 m is 1.5 million times.) Sources: [8, 1]

  • Home zoom

Activity, step by step

  1. 8 min The three small units

    Milli means one thousandth, micro one millionth and nano one billionth. So 1 mm = 1000 micrometres and 1 micrometre = 1000 nanometres. Anchor with two real sizes: a pin head is about 2 mm across and a red blood cell about 8 micrometres. Sources: [1, 2]

  2. 12 min Field of view

    The field of view is the width of the circle you see. Field of view = field number divided by objective magnification. This virtual microscope has a 10x eyepiece with field number 18 mm, and objectives of 4x, 10x, 40x and 100x (40x, 100x, 400x and 1000x in total). Students calculate the field of view for each objective, then switch magnification on the Paramecium slide and compare with the scale bar. Sources: [3, 4]

    • Microscope: Paramecium

  3. 12 min Estimate, then check

    At 400x total (40x objective) the field is 0.45 mm, or 450 micrometres. Students estimate what fraction of the field a Paramecium fills and multiply. They compare their estimate with the field guide range of 170 to 330 micrometres, then repeat with bacteria at 1000x (rods 1 to 4 micrometres long on average). Sources: [3, 5, 6]

    • Microscope: Paramecium
    • Microscope: Bacteria

  4. 8 min Magnification is not everything

    Magnification makes an object look bigger; resolving power is the ability to see two close things as separate. Student light microscopes magnify up to about 400 times (about 1000 with oil immersion) and resolve about 200 nanometres; electron microscopes magnify about 100,000 times. Ask: could a light microscope show the head of ATP synthase, about 9 nanometres across? Zoom the home journey to that level to check. Sources: [2, 7]

    • Home zoom: ATP synthase

  5. 5 min Exit ticket

    Students answer the exit ticket.

Discussion questions

  1. Why do bacteria still look tiny even at 1000x?
  2. Your estimate and the field guide disagree. List two reasons that could explain it.
  3. When would a scientist choose an electron microscope over a light microscope, and what would they give up?

Exit ticket

With the 10x objective the field of view is 1.8 mm. A cell stretches across one tenth of the field. How long is it in micrometres?

Look for: 1.8 mm / 10 = 0.18 mm = 180 micrometres. Sources: [3, 1]

Differentiation

Support

  • Give a filled-in conversion ladder (mm, micrometre, nanometre) with arrows marked multiply by 1000 and divide by 1000.
  • Let students use the on-screen scale bar instead of the formula for the estimate step.
  • Work the 4x objective row together before pairs do the rest.

Extension

  • How many 5 micrometre yeast cells would fit across the 1000x field of view?
  • Write the field of view at each objective in scientific notation in metres.
