Lesson 2 · 50 min

Organelles and their jobs

A jigsaw tour of the animal cell, then students follow one protein from the nucleus to the Golgi and compare animal and plant cells.

  • Middle school (NGSS MS)
  • Ontario Grade 12 Biology (SBI4U)
  • Cells

Student worksheet Answer key (teachers)

Learning goals

  • Match eight organelles to their jobs.
  • Describe how the nucleus, ribosomes, endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi work together to make and ship a protein.
  • Name three parts a plant cell has that an animal cell does not.

You need

  • One device per group of four
  • Student worksheet 2

5 min Hook

Open the animal cell with labels on (press L). Ask: if this cell were a factory, which part would be the office that holds the plans, and which would be the power plant? Collect guesses and keep them on the board to check later.

  • Animal cell

Activity, step by step

  1. 15 min Organelle jigsaw

    Give each group member two parts to research on the animal cell: nucleus and ribosomes; endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus; mitochondria and lysosomes; cell membrane and cytoskeleton. Each student reads the part card, writes the job in their own words in the worksheet table, then teaches it to the group. Check the hook guesses: the nucleus holds the DNA, and mitochondria make ATP. Sources: [1, 2, 3]

    • Nucleus
    • Ribosomes
    • Endoplasmic reticulum
    • Golgi apparatus
    • Mitochondria
    • Lysosomes
    • Cell membrane
    • Cytoskeleton

  2. 10 min Follow one protein

    As a class, trace a protein's route on the projector. The nucleus holds the DNA and directs protein making. Ribosomes join amino acids in the order set by messenger RNA; some sit on the rough endoplasmic reticulum. The ER modifies proteins and sends them in small sacs called vesicles to the Golgi, which sorts, tags and packages them and ships them on in new vesicles. Students draw the route as a flow chart. Sources: [1, 2]

    • Endoplasmic reticulum
    • Golgi apparatus

  3. 10 min Plant cell or animal cell?

    Open the plant cell. It has most of the same organelles, plus a cell wall (mostly cellulose), chloroplasts that capture light, and a large central vacuole that controls how much water the cell holds. Most plant cells do not have lysosomes; the vacuole does that digesting. Students complete a Venn diagram. Sources: [1]

    • Plant cell
    • Central vacuole

  4. 5 min Shape fits the job

    Each pair picks one specialised cell and writes one sentence linking a part to its job: a neuron passes information from the dendrites, across the cell body and down the axon; a mature red blood cell has no nucleus, which leaves room for hemoglobin; a muscle cell has a very high concentration of mitochondria. Sources: [4, 5, 1]

    • Neuron
    • Red blood cell
    • Muscle cell mitochondria

  5. 5 min Exit ticket

    Students answer the exit ticket.

Discussion questions

  1. Why does it help that some ribosomes sit right on the endoplasmic reticulum?
  2. A plant wilts on a hot day. Which organelle explains what you see?
  3. Which cell part would you expect to find in large numbers in a cell that does a lot of work? Why?

Exit ticket

Suppose a cell's lysosomes stopped working. Predict one problem the cell would have.

Look for: Worn-out organelles and large molecules such as proteins, complex sugars, lipids and nucleic acids would not be broken down, because lysosomes hold the enzymes that digest them. Sources: [1]

Differentiation

Support

  • Give each student a half-filled organelle table with the names already written in.
  • Use the analogy of a factory only as a starting point; ask students where it breaks down.
  • Turn on Labels (press L) so the colour key numbers match the list.

Extension

  • Explain why a red blood cell, which has no nucleus, cannot make new proteins for itself.
  • Compare the bacterium with the plant cell: which parts do both have, and why might a bacterium manage without a nucleus?
