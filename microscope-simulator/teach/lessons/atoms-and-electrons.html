Lesson 6 · 50 min

Atoms and electrons

Students use the atom viewer to count protons, neutrons and electrons, draw shell diagrams, find the pattern in group 1, and compare two models of the atom.

  • High school (NGSS HS)
  • Ontario Grade 9 (SNC1W)
  • Atoms
  • Elements

Student worksheet Answer key (teachers)

Learning goals

  • Give the location, relative mass and charge of protons, neutrons and electrons.
  • Draw a shell (Bohr-Rutherford) diagram for an element from its electrons per shell.
  • Link an element's group in the periodic table to the number of electrons in its outer shell.
  • Name one strength and one limit of the shell model and the orbital model.

You need

  • One device per pair
  • Student worksheet 6

5 min Hook

Zoom the home journey to its last level, a phosphorus atom. An atom is on the order of 0.1 nanometres across and its nucleus is about 100,000 times smaller. Ask: if an atom were 100 metres across, how big would the nucleus be? (100 m divided by 100,000 is 1 mm.) Sources: [1, 4]

  • Home zoom: atom

Activity, step by step

  1. 10 min Inside the nucleus

    Protons (charge 1+) and neutrons (no charge) sit in the nucleus and have almost the same mass; electrons (charge 1-) are outside it and about 1800 of them weigh as much as one proton. The number of protons is the atomic number, and a neutral atom has the same number of electrons. Open the nucleus view: carbon-12 has 6 protons and 6 neutrons; sodium-23 has 11 protons and 12 neutrons. Sources: [1, 2]

    • Atom viewer: carbon nucleus
    • Atom viewer: sodium nucleus

  2. 12 min Shells

    Switch to Shells. Pairs record electrons per shell for H, C, O, Na, Mg, Cl and K and draw two shell diagrams. Values: H 1; C 2, 4; O 2, 6; Na 2, 8, 1; Mg 2, 8, 2; Cl 2, 8, 7; K 2, 8, 8, 1. Use Previous and Next element, or the [ and ] keys. Sources: [2]

    • Atom viewer: shells
    • Sodium shells

  3. 10 min The group 1 pattern

    Compare lithium (2, 1), sodium (2, 8, 1) and potassium (2, 8, 8, 1): each has one electron in its outer shell, and each sits in group 1. Then open the 3D landscape with column height set to first ionization energy: group 1 metals are the low valleys (Na 5.1 eV, K 4.3 eV) and noble gases the peaks (He 24.6 eV). Read the sodium card: it tarnishes within seconds in air and reacts strongly with water. Sources: [2, 3]

    • 3D landscape: ionization energy
    • Sodium card

  4. 8 min Two models, one atom

    Show oxygen in Orbitals and in Shells. The viewer labels the shell view a simplified picture, because real electrons do not travel in circles, and the orbital view a teaching model built from hydrogen-like orbital shapes. Students write one thing each model shows well and one thing it gets wrong or leaves out. Sources: [2]

    • Atom viewer: oxygen orbitals
    • Atom viewer: oxygen shells

  5. 5 min Exit ticket

    Students answer the exit ticket.

Discussion questions

  1. Every carbon atom has 6 protons, but not every carbon atom has 6 neutrons. Which number decides that it is carbon?
  2. Why do lithium, sodium and potassium behave alike?
  3. Why do scientists keep using the shell model if real electrons do not orbit in circles?

Exit ticket

Chlorine has 17 protons. How many electrons does a neutral chlorine atom have, and how many are in its outer shell?

Look for: 17 electrons, arranged 2, 8, 7, so 7 in the outer shell. Sources: [1, 2]

Differentiation

Support

  • Give a blank shell diagram template with rings already drawn.
  • Provide a three-row table: particle, where, charge, relative mass, with one row filled in.
  • Use the viewer's text description, which lists protons, neutrons and electrons per shell.

Extension

  • Predict the outer-shell electrons for magnesium and calcium, then check in the viewer. What do they share?
  • Research how one experiment (for example Rutherford's gold foil) changed the model of the atom.
