Lesson 8 · 60 min

DNA to protein

Students read a real DNA structure, trace a protein's route through the cell, transcribe and translate a short gene by hand, and connect codon counts to real proteins in the explorer.

  • High school (NGSS HS)
  • Ontario Grade 11 Biology (SBI3U)
  • Ontario Grade 12 Biology (SBI4U)
  • DNA and genetics
  • Molecules
  • Cells

Student worksheet Answer key (teachers)

Learning goals

  • Describe the structure of DNA and its base-pairing rules.
  • Explain transcription (DNA to mRNA) and translation (mRNA to protein) and where each happens.
  • Use codons, the start codon and stop codons to find how many amino acids a short gene codes for.
  • Explain how inserting or deleting one base changes the protein.

You need

  • One device per pair
  • Student worksheet 8

5 min Hook

Open green fluorescent protein. Its glowing part forms by itself from three amino acids at the centre of a barrel, and scientists use GFP as a marker to see where genes are switched on. Ask: if the glow comes from the order of amino acids, where is that order written down? Sources: [11]

  • Green fluorescent protein

Activity, step by step

  1. 10 min Read the DNA

    Open DNA. Two strands wind into a double helix with the sugar and phosphate backbone outside and the bases pairing across the middle, A with T and G with C. Light up the phosphorus atoms to see the backbone. This crystal is a 12 base pair piece with the strand CGCGAATTCGCG. Students write the partner strand. Sources: [1, 2, 3]

    • DNA
    • DNA: phosphorus lit up

  2. 10 min The route through the cell

    The nucleus holds the DNA. Transcription makes a messenger RNA copy, written with A, C, G and U. Ribosomes read the mRNA and join amino acids in the order it sets; some sit on the rough endoplasmic reticulum, and proteins can move on to the Golgi to be packaged. Sources: [4, 5, 6]

    • Nucleus
    • Ribosomes
    • Golgi apparatus

  3. 20 min Decode a gene by hand

    Give the template strand TAC CGT AAA GGC ATT. The mRNA pairs with the template, with U in place of T (A to U, T to A, G to C, C to G). Each amino acid is set by a three-letter codon; there are 64 possible codons. AUG is the start codon and codes for methionine; UAA, UAG and UGA are stop codons. Students transcribe, mark start and stop, and count the amino acids. Then delete the first G of GGC and repeat to see the reading frame shift. Sources: [6, 7, 8]

  4. 10 min Real proteins, real lengths

    Open insulin (chain A 21 amino acids, chain B 30) and lysozyme (one chain of 129). Students calculate how many coding nucleotides each chain needs at three per amino acid, plus three more for a stop codon. Sources: [9, 10, 6, 8]

    • Insulin
    • Lysozyme

  5. 5 min Exit ticket

    Students answer the exit ticket.

Discussion questions

  1. Why can't codons be two letters long? Use the numbers 16 and 64.
  2. Deleting one base changed every amino acid after it. Why would deleting three bases do less damage?
  3. The DNA in the explorer reads the same on both strands. Is that common, or special to this piece? How could you check?

Exit ticket

An mRNA reads AUG UUU GGC UGA. How many amino acids does it code for, and which codon stops it?

Look for: Three amino acids (AUG, UUU, GGC); UGA is the stop codon. Sources: [6, 8]

Differentiation

Support

  • Give a pairing card: template A to mRNA U, T to A, G to C, C to G.
  • Pre-split the template strand into groups of three.
  • Do the first codon together on the board.

Extension

  • Write a 15-letter template strand of your own with a start and a stop codon, and swap with a partner to decode.
  • Find the start codon AUG in the OpenStax genetic code table and list two other amino acids by their codons.
