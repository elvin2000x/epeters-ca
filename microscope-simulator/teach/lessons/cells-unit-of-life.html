Lesson 1 · 45 min

Cells are the unit of life

Students meet whole organisms that are a single cell, compare a bacterium with an animal cell, and use what they see to argue for the three ideas of cell theory.

  • Middle school (NGSS MS)
  • Ontario Grade 11 Biology (SBI3U)
  • Cells
  • Microorganisms

Student worksheet Answer key (teachers)

Learning goals

  • State the three ideas of cell theory.
  • Tell an organism made of one cell from one made of many cells, using examples from the explorer.
  • Compare where the DNA sits in a bacterium and in an animal cell.

You need

  • Projector or one device per pair
  • Student worksheet 1 (one per student)

5 min Hook

Project the home zoom stopped at its first level, a Paramecium in pond water. Ask: is this one thing alive, or is it a part of something alive? Take three answers, then reveal that the whole organism is a single cell, 170 to 330 micrometres long and covered in 10,000 to 14,000 cilia. Sources: [2, 9]

  • Home zoom: Paramecium

Activity, step by step

  1. 8 min Cell theory in three lines

    Write the three ideas of cell theory on the board: one or more cells make up every living thing; the cell is the basic unit of life; new cells come from cells that already exist. Name Schleiden and Schwann (late 1830s) and Virchow, who added to the theory later. Students copy the three ideas onto the worksheet in their own words. Sources: [1]

  2. 12 min Whole organisms that are one cell

    In pairs, students open each slide in the virtual microscope and fill the worksheet table: name, one cell or many, size from the field guide, and one thing it does. Paramecium is 170 to 330 micrometres long, Euglena is up to about 100 micrometres long and makes food with chloroplasts, and baker's yeast is about 5 micrometres across. Sources: [2, 3, 4, 5]

    • Microscope: Paramecium
    • Microscope: Euglena
    • Microscope: Yeast

  3. 10 min Bacterium or animal cell?

    Open the bacterial cell and tap the nucleoid, then open the animal cell and tap the nucleus. A bacterium has no nucleus, so its DNA sits in a region called the nucleoid; an animal cell keeps its DNA inside a nucleus wrapped in a double membrane. Every prokaryote has DNA, ribosomes, a cell membrane and a cell wall. Students list what both cells share and what only the animal cell has. Sources: [6, 7]

    • Bacterial cell: nucleoid
    • Animal cell: nucleus

  4. 5 min Organisms made of many cells

    Show the muscle cell. A single skeletal muscle cell can be up to about 30 cm long in the upper leg and holds many nuclei. Contrast it with a red blood cell, about 8 micrometres across. Cells of one kind that join up and share a job form a tissue. Sources: [8, 1]

    • Muscle cell
    • Red blood cell

  5. 5 min Exit ticket

    Students answer the exit ticket on a card or the back of the worksheet.

Discussion questions

  1. Yeast is one cell. Is it a cell, an organism, or both? What makes you say so?
  2. In the pond view, a Paramecium splits in two and yeast grows a bud. Which idea of cell theory does that support?
  3. A muscle cell has many nuclei. Why might a very long cell that makes lots of protein need more than one?

Exit ticket

Write the three ideas of cell theory. Then give one thing you saw today that supports one of them.

Look for: One or more cells make up all living things; the cell is the basic unit of life; new cells come from existing cells. Example evidence: a Paramecium or a yeast cell is a whole living organism that is one cell, or a Paramecium dividing in two in the pond view. Sources: [1, 10, 11]

Differentiation

Support

  • Give a word bank: cell, organism, nucleus, nucleoid, DNA, membrane, wall.
  • Pair a confident reader with a student who needs the text read aloud; every explorer page has a text description of the view.
  • Fill in the first row of the table together on the projector.

Extension

  • How many times longer is a 240 micrometre Paramecium than an 8 micrometre red blood cell?
  • Find out what Rudolf Virchow added to cell theory and write two sentences on it.
